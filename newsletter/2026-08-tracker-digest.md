# A committee voted for BPC-157. Almost nothing changed.

You subscribed for meaningful changes in the regulatory record—not manufactured
urgency. This is the first update, and it will only arrive when something
actually changes.

**What changed:** An FDA advisory committee recommended six peptide-related
bulk drug substances for possible inclusion on the 503A Bulks List.

**What did not change:** None became FDA-approved, automatically lawful to
compound, or validated as retail research products.

**What happens next:** FDA must decide whether to proceed through the formal
regulatory process. The committee’s vote is advisory.

---

## What happened

On **23–24 July 2026**, the FDA’s Pharmacy Compounding Advisory Committee
considered seven peptide-related bulk drug substances for possible inclusion on
the 503A Bulks List—a federal pathway that can permit qualifying substances to
be used in certain patient-specific pharmacy compounding when statutory and
regulatory conditions are met.

The committee recommended:

**BPC-157 · TB-500 · KPV · MOTS-c · Epitalon · Semax**

It declined to recommend emideltide, also known as DSIP.

Three details matter more than the headline.

**The margins were narrow.** The votes included 8–6 margins with one abstention
for BPC-157, TB-500 and KPV, and a 7–5 vote for MOTS-c. These were not consensus
decisions.

**The committee voted against FDA staff recommendations.** FDA reviewers
recommended against inclusion, citing unresolved questions involving
characterization, quality, safety and evidence of effectiveness.

**Identity was part of the problem.** FDA staff raised concerns about whether
the substances were defined and characterized consistently enough to support a
reliable evaluation. Before safety, effectiveness or manufacturing quality can
be assessed, the substance itself must be clearly identified.

---

## What it does not mean

A committee recommendation is not a rule change.

The committee does not bind FDA. The vote did not add these substances to the
503A Bulks List, approve them as drugs, or create broad permission to manufacture
or sell them.

Any actual change requires further FDA action through the applicable regulatory
process, and the final outcome can differ from the committee’s recommendation.

**Nothing about what a consumer could lawfully purchase changed on July 24.**

The vote also does not prove that a vial sold online is FDA-approved, lawful,
clinically effective, correctly identified, sterile, accurately dosed or
properly manufactured. Those remain separate questions.

When a seller presents this vote as validation—or worse, as approval—that claim
should be treated cautiously. It is the most predictable marketing narrative to
come out of the meeting.

## Why the identity issue matters

FDA’s concern reflects the same analytical problem this site repeatedly
emphasizes:

You cannot properly evaluate a substance whose identity is not pinned down.

A reported purity percentage describes the sample and method used for a
particular test. By itself, it does not establish that the material is the
molecule claimed on the label, that the amount is accurate, that the product is
sterile, or that harmful contaminants are absent.

The regulators encountered a version of the same verification problem faced by
anyone looking at a vial and a certificate of analysis. They simply have more
resources and better instruments.

## Also moving

- **Category 2 removals** — BPC-157, TB-500 and CJC-1295 were removed from the
  flagged-risk category in April, but were not moved into Category 1. Removal
  from one category should not be read as permission, approval or a finding of
  safety.

- **Proposed 503B exclusions** — FDA proposed excluding semaglutide,
  tirzepatide and liraglutide from the 503B Bulks List. The public comment
  period closed on June 29, 2026. A final determination has not yet been
  announced.

- **Telehealth warning letters** — FDA announced 30 warning letters concerning
  false or misleading marketing of compounded GLP-1 products, particularly
  claims that could imply equivalence with FDA-approved drugs.

## One housekeeping note

Every compound page now carries citations. Material claims link to the study,
trial or regulatory document behind them, and cited DOIs were checked against
the DOI registry to confirm that they resolve to the paper identified.

Two GHK-Cu citations are worth highlighting because they do not support the
usual promotional narrative.

A controlled trial of topical GHK-Cu after laser resurfacing found **no
objective improvement** in wrinkles or overall skin quality, although
patient-reported satisfaction was higher.

Several widely cited GHK-Cu reviews were also authored by the founder of a
company that sells GHK-Cu skincare. That conflict of interest does not
automatically invalidate the papers, but readers should know about it when the
same publications are presented as independent support.

does not claim medical, pharmaceutical, laboratory or legal authority.
